The Math Behind The Spins

Roulette house edge, variance, and betting systems

The simulator above plays out one session. This section explains why those sessions behave the way they do, so the numbers on screen make sense instead of feeling random.

Where the roulette house edge comes from

Roulette pays as if the zeros were not on the wheel. A straight-up number pays 35:1, which would be an even proposition on a 36-pocket wheel. The zeros are the entire house edge.

European roulette has 37 pockets — 1 through 36 plus a single zero — so a straight-up bet wins once every 37 spins on average:

EV = (1/37 × 35) − (36/37 × 1) = −0.0270 → house edge 2.70%

American roulette adds a double zero for 38 pockets, so the same bet wins once every 38 spins:

EV = (1/38 × 35) − (37/38 × 1) = −0.0526 → house edge 5.26%

That one extra pocket nearly doubles the long-run cost of playing. If you have a choice between wheels, the single-zero wheel is the cheaper game for identical bets.

One exception worth knowing: the American five-number bet on 0, 00, 1, 2, 3 pays 6:1 and carries a 7.89% house edge, making it the most expensive standard bet on either wheel.

Variance: why the same edge feels completely different

Every standard bet on a European wheel carries the same 2.70% edge. What changes between bets is variance — how violently the bankroll moves while that edge grinds away. Straight-up bets and red/black bets cost the same per dollar wagered over time, but they produce very different sessions.

“Swing per spin” is the standard deviation of a one-unit bet. A straight-up bet moves almost six times as much per spin as red/black, which is why inside bets produce long dry spells punctuated by sudden spikes, while outside bets drift up and down.

Over a short session, variance is much larger than the edge. Flat-betting $10 on red for 100 spins on a European wheel gives an expected loss of about $27, but a typical swing of roughly $100 in either direction. That is why a losing game produces winning sessions so often — and why a good night says nothing about the underlying math.

European, $10 on redExpected lossTypical swing
100 spins$27about $100

10,000 spins$2,703about $1,000

Notice the crossover. At 100 spins the swing dwarfs the edge and almost anything can happen. By 10,000 spins the expected loss is nearly three times the typical swing, and the house edge has become the dominant term. Variance hides the edge in the short run; it cannot outrun it in the long run.

Martingale vs flat betting

The Martingale doubles the bet after every loss so that a single win recovers everything lost plus one base unit. It does work most of the time, which is exactly what makes it worth understanding. What it does not do is change the house edge — each individual spin still pays 2.70% or 5.26% to the house regardless of what the previous spins did.

What the Martingale actually changes is the shape of the risk. It trades a large number of small wins for a small chance of a very large loss. Starting from $10 on an even-money bet:

Consecutive lossesNext bet requiredTotal risked so far
1$20$10
2$40$30
3$80$70
4$160$150
5$320$310
6$640$630
7$1,280$1,270
8$2,560$2,550

Win at any rung and the profit is still $10. That is the trade: you are risking $2,550 in cumulative action to win a single base unit, and the bet size doubles every step. Eight consecutive even-money losses happen about 0.48% of the time on a European wheel and 0.59% on an American wheel — rare per attempt, but not rare across a session. Over 250 spins, at least one run of eight losses shows up roughly 44% of the time on European and 50% on American.

Two hard limits make the ladder finite in practice: the table maximum and your bankroll. When either one stops you from placing the next bet, the sequence ends with the full accumulated loss and no chance to recover it. A progression cannot borrow from a bankroll you do not have.

Flat betting keeps the same amount at risk on every spin, which makes the session easier to read and the downside easier to plan for. Neither approach removes the house edge. Roulette simulator FAQ

Use the simulator to understand roulette risk, not to predict the next result.

Can this roulette simulator predict the next spin?

No. The simulator is for testing roulette bets, bankroll pressure, and variance. It cannot predict a real roulette spin or guarantee a winning session.

Should I test European or American roulette?

European roulette has one zero, while American roulette has zero and double zero. The extra double zero raises the house edge, so comparing both wheel types helps show the cost difference.

Does a betting system change roulette odds?

No. A betting system changes the size and pattern of bets, but it does not change the wheel odds or remove the house edge.
